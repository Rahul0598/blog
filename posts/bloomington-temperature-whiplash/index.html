Snip, snap: Bloomington’s temperature whiplash

Date: Oct 18, 2026, Last Modified: Oct 18, 2026
Tags [ weather data-viz ]

It’s October 18, and it’s suddenly cold. Just a few days ago, I was enjoying the sunshine and warm air. It almost felt like summer! Now it’s hard to step outside without a jacket, and I’m mad at myself for not spending more time outdoors while it was warm.

Then, today, I saw this post about how it's going to be warmer again next week:

Read the post on Facebook: warmer-than-normal temperatures are expected to return to the Midwest and Plains after the chilly weekend.

I was reminded of Michael Scott in The Office. I’m happy it’s warming up again, but man: snip, snap, snip, snap.

That got me wondering: how often has Bloomington’s weather swung back and forth like this? And are these swings becoming more common?

To explore those questions, I used daily high temperatures from the Bloomington IU weather station within the city. I chose IU for its longer historical record, which begins in 1895.

The chart below looks for sharp temperature swings followed by a reversal within a few days. Move the threshold to explore when these cycles happened and how intense they were.

Methods

A cycle is warm-cold-warm or cold-warm-cold, with the threshold met on both temperature changes. Intensity is the smaller of the two absolute changes: 71 → 27 → 76°F changes by 44°F, then 49°F, so its intensity is 44°F. This ensures both changes are substantial. Overlapping candidates are consolidated, strongest first.

Full-cycle span is the elapsed time from the first temperature to the final temperature, including both changes. Four days cooling followed by five days warming spans nine days. The median summarizes all detected cycles in the record under the current settings.

R² is the fraction of variation between annual values explained by the fitted curve. Linear trends are solid when their 95% change interval excludes zero, dashed otherwise.

The default is a 27°F (15°C) minimum change in each direction.

With the default threshold of 27°F (15°C) in both directions, and at most three days for each change:

  • Cycles are becoming more frequent: the fitted annual count rises by about 2.1 cycles between 1897 and 2025.
  • The intensity trend is less clear: the fitted mean intensity rises by about 0.8°F between 1897 and 2025, but its uncertainty includes no change.
  • Warm-cold-warm is more common than cold-warm-cold: 133 versus 101 cycles.
  • The strongest complete cycles tie in 2026 and 2019: 71 → 27 → 76°F on March 16-21, 2026, and 46 → 2 → 48°F on January 29-February 3, 2019. Both have a 44°F minimum change, with two days of cooling and three of warming.
  • The biggest single change within three days happened in 1972: a 59°F rise, from -7 to 52°F on January 16-19.

Bloomington has a long history of snip-snap weather, including bigger single swings decades ago. With this definition, sharp reversals are becoming more frequent.

Local reporting puts these episodes in context:

  • The B Square: “Late January cold sets record for Bloomington” (January 31, 2019). Reports the IU station’s 2°F daily high, the cold turning point in the 2019 cycle above. It documents the cold phase of that reversal.
  • WRTV: “March weather madness: From snow to flooding to storms” (March 16-17, 2026). A downtown Indianapolis resident, walking in snow, recalls sunbathing in shorts just two days earlier. The story explicitly calls the conditions “weather whiplash.”
  • WRTV: “Why have there been so many wild weather swings in March in Indianapolis?” (March 25, 2026). An NWS meteorologist discusses Indianapolis’s March 22 record high of 88°F and a rare 50°F spread down to 38°F. That comparison uses a high and a low, whereas the charts here compare daily highs.
