Snip, snap: Bloomington’s temperature whiplash

Date: Oct 18, 2026, Last Modified: Oct 18, 2026
Tags [ weather data-viz ]

It’s October 18, and it’s suddenly cold. Just a few days ago, I was enjoying the sunshine and warm air. It almost felt like summer! Now it’s hard to step outside without a jacket, and I’m mad at myself for not spending more time outdoors while it was warm.

Then, today, I saw this post about how it's going to be warmer again next week:

Read the post on Facebook: warmer-than-normal temperatures are expected to return to the Midwest and Plains after the chilly weekend.

I was reminded of Michael Scott in The Office. I’m happy it’s warming up again, but man: snip, snap, snip, snap.

That got me wondering: how often has Bloomington’s weather swung back and forth like this? And are these swings becoming more common?

To find out, I used daily high temperatures from the Bloomington IU weather station. Its record starts in 1895.

I looked for a sharp change in daily highs followed by a change back. By default, each change must be at least 27°F and take no more than three days. Each point below is one such reversal. Move the threshold to explore other sizes.

With the default settings, the strongest cycles tie in 2019 and 2026. Daily highs went 46 → 2 → 48°F on January 29–February 3, 2019, and 71 → 27 → 76°F on March 16–21, 2026. Both cycles had a smaller change of 44°F.

With those same settings, warm-cold-warm cycles outnumber cold-warm-cold cycles, 133 to 101. The largest single change within three days was a 59°F rise in January 1972, from −7°F to 52°F.

Methods

A cycle is warm-cold-warm or cold-warm-cold, with the threshold met on both temperature changes. Intensity is the smaller of the two absolute changes: 71 → 27 → 76°F changes by 44°F, then 49°F, so its intensity is 44°F. This ensures both changes are substantial. Overlapping candidates are consolidated, strongest first.

Full-cycle span is the elapsed time from the first temperature to the final temperature, including both changes. Four days cooling followed by five days warming spans nine days. The median summarizes all detected cycles in the record under the current settings.

R² is the fraction of variation between annual values explained by the fitted curve. Linear trends are solid when their 95% change interval excludes zero, dashed otherwise.

The default is a 27°F (15°C) minimum change in each direction.

How return periods are calculated

For each eligible year, take the strongest detected reversal cycle, assigned to the year of its turning point. For a chosen intensity, count years containing at least one cycle that meets or exceeds it. Divide that count by the total eligible years to get the observed annual exceedance fraction p; the empirical annual return period is 1/p. Years with no qualifying cycle remain in the denominator. This measures recurrence of years with a cycle, rather than the average spacing between individual cycles. See the NWS explanation of annual exceedance and recurrence for that distinction.

The intensity threshold and maximum days for each change update this panel. It uses the same full-record years with at least 95% valid observations as the trend fits, including years with zero cycles. Cycles in excluded years remain visible in the timeline but do not enter these estimates; for example, the 2019 and 2026 peaks are excluded. Missing observations can still hide a year's strongest cycle. No probability distribution is fitted and no return period is extrapolated beyond observed intensities. With zero exceeding years, a finite return period cannot be estimated.

The comparison above uses the first and last 30-calendar-year windows anchored to the first and last eligible years. Each point in the history chart repeats that calculation for the preceding 30 calendar years, ending in the plotted year. At least 20 of those years must meet the 95% daily-data coverage rule; other windows are gaps. Counts are divided by the number of eligible years in that window, not by 30 automatically. The time brush does not change these windows. The comparison has its own intensity selector and shares the maximum-days setting above; the folded full-record curve follows the timeline's intensity threshold.

The table's approximate 95% intervals use the Wilson binomial method. They assume independent years with a common probability within each window, and do not account for serial dependence, station changes or missing extremes. Overlapping rolling windows share observations and are not independent samples. These intervals are descriptive guides, rather than a formal test of a trend or a change between eras.

A 10-year annual return period means a historical exceedance fraction of 10%, not a regular ten-year schedule. Treating that fraction as a probability for a future year would assume stable conditions; this record's changing frequency makes that assumption questionable. The curve is descriptive and has no confidence band.

What those weeks felt like

Local reports describe some of the cold and warm spells behind these swings:

  • The B Square: “Late January cold sets record for Bloomington” (January 31, 2019). Reports the IU station’s 2°F daily high, the cold turning point in the 2019 reversal above.
  • WRTV: “March weather madness: From snow to flooding to storms” (March 16-17, 2026). A downtown Indianapolis resident, walking in snow, recalls sunbathing in shorts just two days earlier. The story explicitly calls the conditions “weather whiplash.”
  • WRTV: “Why have there been so many wild weather swings in March in Indianapolis?” (March 25, 2026). An NWS meteorologist discusses Indianapolis’s March 22 record high of 88°F and a rare 50°F spread down to 38°F. That comparison uses a high and a low, whereas the charts here compare daily highs.

Comparison methods and sources

IU provides official daily maxima in GHCN-Daily, rather than an hourly GHCNh series. KBMG uses GHCNh observations, downloaded October 18, 2026. Its highest accepted reading is calculated in the same preceding-day 7 a.m. to reporting-day 7 a.m. window. UTC timestamps are converted using America/Indiana/Indianapolis, including daylight-saving changes.

Both series are rounded to whole °F. Airport days require at least 90% hourly coverage, no gap over three hours, and readings within the official daily temperature bounds (2°F tolerance). A year must have valid paired highs on at least 95% of its days. Every comparison uses the same dates at both stations; incomplete years and missing data are excluded.

The lower chart averages |T(t) - T(t - n)| for the selected number of calendar days, requiring valid highs on every intervening date at both stations. It measures a single change, rather than a complete reversal cycle. Overall averages weight each paired day or change equally.

These small differences do not establish a citywide heat-island effect. IU’s official daily maximum can differ from the highest sampled airport reading, and station instruments, moves and exposure differ. Daily highs also leave out nighttime warmth, when urban heat retention can be more pronounced. EPA heat-island context · Download paired summaries and processing notes.

These two stations don’t describe every part of Bloomington. Daily highs also leave out nighttime temperatures.

What I take from this

With the default definition, reversals have become more frequent at the IU station. Their average size shows no clear trend. There are too few reversals of at least 40°F in both directions to draw a firm conclusion about how their frequency has changed.
