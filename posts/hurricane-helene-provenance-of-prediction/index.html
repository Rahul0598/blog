Hurricane Helene and the Provenance of Its Prediction

Date: Oct 2, 2024, Last Modified: Oct 2, 2024
Tags [ climate weather gis ]
  1. In December 2023, the TSR (Tropical Storm Risk) predicted tropical storm activity over the Atlantic to be 30% above 1991-2020 30-year norm, and 50% above the 1950-2023 year long-term norm (with large uncertainties).
  2. On 4th April 2024, the CSU’s forecast ranked 2024 as the 5th most active hurricane season in the satellite era (1966 - present). Citing warming sea temperatures and the development of La Nina over the summer (more details predicting more hurricanes due to trade winds and vertical wind shear).
    1. 2005 was the most active, with four hurricanes reaching Cat 5.
    2. 2004 and 2017 shared something in common – two of the longest duration Category 5 hurricanes on record. Hurricane Irma (2017 - 3.25 days) and Hurricane Ivan (2004 - 3 days)
  3. On 8th April 2024, the TSR updated their hurricane activity forecast to 70% above the 1991-2020 norm.
  4. May 23, 2024 - NOAA predicts above normal Atlantic hurricane season, citing La Nina and warmer-than-average ocean temperatures.
  1. 26th September 2024 - Hurricane Helene makes landfall in Florida. Rapidly intensified from Cat 1 - 4.
    1. With an ACE of 1.3 and duration of 3 days, indicate that it was relatively weak (and short lived) compared to other storms over the Atlantic.
    2. North Carolina (along with parts across Atlanta - southern Appalachian region) had already received over 4-9inches of rain. These wet conditions made the hurricane last 3 days (24th - 27th Sept), longer than usual due to evaporation over land.
    3. Most of the damage caused by Helene was in places less familiar with tropical storms. Asheville, NC - a small city of population 95000 was highly impacted due to compounding effects of the storm - prior rainfall, overflowing rivers. These repairs could take at least weeks to months.
    4. Poweroutage.us still shows 100s of thousands of people without power (As of Oct 2, 5AM).
    5. FCC - cable TV/FM/AM stations reported out of service and 100s of thousands of users affected and out of service.
    6. Moody’s estimates $20-34B economic cost of the hurricane.
    7. Vox gives a good summary of the storm.

      On economic damage: The dollar value, however, doesn’t tell the whole story. Damage estimates are typically based on insurance claims, but with rising premiums across the country, more homes and businesses are not seeking financial protection. The insured value of a property doesn’t directly translate into suffering, either. A multimillion-dollar coastal vacation home getting swept down a hillside will register as a higher loss on an insurance company’s balance sheet than a destroyed mobile home that’s the sole residence and store of wealth for a family.

      On flooding in NC: The simplest reason is that Helene was huge, stretching more than more than 400 miles wide, so its impacts — the wind and rain — reached well beyond the eye. Most hurricanes are around 300 miles in diameter.

  2. On impacts of climate change:
    1. Risser, North and Wehner - Climate change may have caused as much as 50% more rainfall during Hurricane Helene in some parts of Georgia and the Carolinas.
    2. CSU graph shows 3 (2020/21/23) out of the last 4 years had the highest number of named storms.
    3. GFDL and NYTimes -
      1. “Storms are expected to cause more coastal flooding because of sea-level rise. They can deliver larger downpours because warmer air can carry more moisture. And they’re becoming more likely to intensify rapidly because they form over warmer seawater.”
      2. “Scientists do not expect the total number of hurricanes to increase globally as the planet heats up. But among the storms that do form, a larger share is expected to intensify to Category 4 or 5,”
      3. “In summary, it is premature to conclude with high confidence that increasing atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations from human activities have had a detectable impact on Atlantic basin hurricane activity, although increasing greenhouse gases are strongly linked to global warming… Human activities may have already caused other changes in tropical cyclone activity that are not yet detectable due to the small magnitude of these changes compared to estimated natural variability, or due to observational limitations.”
    4. NCA (National climate assessment) - Southeast communities to be hit the most due to threats from climate change (especially the ones hit by Helene).
    5. Climate Atlas shows historical trends of ACE (accumulated cyclone energy)
    6. Coral reefs are vanishing - They act as a defense mechanism by breaking down the intensity of waves along the coasts - “That means climate change is not only making tropical storms more severe, but it can also weaken our natural defenses against them. And this is an important, frightening point: A warming ocean makes storms more dangerous, not just because they’re intensifying faster or dropping more rain but because, in places like the Caribbean, we’re losing resilience that iconic ecosystems provide.”

Hurricane Helene describes (or provides an example of) the impacts that climate change can have on hurricane behavior and severity. It brought down over 40 trillion gallons of rainfall across the southern United States, highlighting the increased precipitation associated with warmer sea temperatures. This massive volume of rain led to catastrophic flooding, particularly in regions like western North Carolina, Georgia, and Tennessee, where infrastructure was not designed to handle such extreme weather events. Hurricanes are also more likely to stall, resulting in prolonged exposure to high winds and rain. This calls for increasing resilience amongst the communities that are stated to be affected the most.
